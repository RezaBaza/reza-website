Everyone is talking about AI transformation. Boards are asking about it. Consultants are selling it. Vendors are promising it will solve everything from customer churn to internal reporting.

But after running workshops and proof-of-concept initiatives across different organisations, I've noticed a clear pattern: **the failures have almost nothing to do with the technology.**

## The real problem is not the AI

When an AI initiative fails — and most do, at least the first time — the postmortem usually reveals one of these:

- Nobody agreed on what problem it was actually solving
- The data it needed was messier than assumed
- The output was technically correct but nobody knew what to do with it
- The team that built it wasn't the team that had to use it
- There was no plan for what happens when it gets something wrong

None of these are AI problems. They are organisational problems that AI makes more visible.

## What actually works

The initiatives I've seen create real, measurable value share a few things in common.

**They start with a specific decision, not a capability.** Not "we want to use AI for customer service" but "we want to reduce the time it takes a support agent to find relevant information from 4 minutes to under 30 seconds." The more concrete the target, the easier it is to know whether you succeeded.

**The people closest to the problem are involved from day one.** The support agents in the example above. The finance team for forecasting. The clinician for a healthcare triage tool. Without them, you build something technically impressive that nobody trusts.

**They define what "wrong" looks like before launch.** Every AI model makes mistakes. The question is not whether it will fail — it is how it fails, how often, and what happens when it does. Organisations that think through this beforehand recover faster and build more trust over time.

**The first version is deliberately small.** A pilot that works for one team, one geography, one use case. Not because ambition is bad, but because learning from something small is cheap, and learning from something large is expensive.

## The hype is not the problem

I do not think the excitement around AI is wrong. The tools are genuinely powerful and the pace of improvement is real. What concerns me is when organisations treat AI as a strategy rather than a tool.

A tool has a job. You pick it because it does that job better than the alternatives, or makes it possible when it was not before. When you start with the tool and work backwards to the job, you get expensive pilots that demonstrate impressiveness but do not change how anyone works.

## A useful question

If your organisation is exploring AI initiatives, here is a question worth sitting with:

*If this AI system were removed tomorrow, what decision or process would get worse — and by how much?*

If you struggle to answer that, the initiative probably needs more work at the problem definition stage before it needs more work at the technology stage.

That is not a criticism. It is where most organisations are, and it is fixable. But it requires being honest about it.

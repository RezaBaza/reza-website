Every few years a national strategy document lands, and most public sector leaders skim the executive summary, nod, and move on. I understand the instinct. But the AI strategy the government published in February 2026 is worth more than a skim, partly because it finally puts numbers and deadlines next to the ambitions. So here is the version I wish someone had handed me: what it says, and what it actually means for the people who have to make it real.

**The headline: less talk about potential, more about plumbing.**

For years the Swedish conversation about AI in the public sector circled the same point — that we have great data, high digital maturity, and we should "do more." The 2026 strategy is more honest. It admits that the bottleneck is not ambition or even technology. It is the boring stuff: data that can't be shared between agencies, procurement processes that take eighteen months, and a shortage of people who can tell a good AI use case from a bad one.

If you read nothing else, read the sections on shared infrastructure. The strategy leans heavily on AI Sweden (ai.se) and Digg (digg.se) as the national engines — Digg for the common digital infrastructure, AI Sweden for applied research and the language models trained on Swedish. The signal is clear: the government does not want 290 municipalities each building their own chatbot from scratch. It wants reusable building blocks.

**What the strategy gets right.**

The most useful thing in the document is its insistence on starting with concrete, narrow problems. I have seen this work. When a municipality begins with "how do we use AI" they end up with a pilot that impresses no one. When they begin with "our building permit backlog is nine months and citizens are furious," they end up somewhere useful.

There are already examples worth copying. Several regions have used machine learning to predict which patients are likely to miss appointments, so they can send a targeted reminder — a small intervention that recovers a surprising amount of clinic capacity. The Swedish Tax Agency and Försäkringskassan have used pattern detection on transaction data to flag suspected fraud, freeing caseworkers to look at the cases that genuinely need a human. None of these are flashy. All of them save real money and real hours. The strategy is right to point at this kind of work rather than at moonshots.

It also takes the language question seriously, which matters more than non-Swedes assume. A general model trained mostly on English will mangle Swedish bureaucratic terminology and, worse, will be confidently wrong about it. The continued investment in Swedish-language models is not nationalism; it is a quality-control decision.

**Where I'd push back, gently.**

The strategy is strong on infrastructure and weak on people. It mentions skills and competence, but the practical reality is that most municipalities cannot hire a data scientist, and if they did, that person would have no one to talk to. The honest answer is shared capacity — pooled analysts across several municipalities, or expertise delivered through the regional structures. I would have liked the strategy to be braver here, because hardware and models without people are just expensive shelfware.

The other gap is governance. The EU AI Act now applies in full, and several common public sector uses — anything touching welfare decisions, education, or law enforcement — fall into the high-risk category. The strategy acknowledges this but treats compliance as a constraint to manage rather than a design discipline to build in early. In my experience it is much cheaper to ask "how will we document and audit this?" before you build than to retrofit it after.

**So what should you actually do?**

If you lead a public organisation, three things. First, inventory your data before you inventory your AI ambitions. You cannot build on data you can't trust or access, and most of the work is here. Second, pick one painful, well-defined process and treat the first AI project as a learning exercise, not a transformation programme. The goal is a team that understands what these tools can and cannot do. Third, lean on the national building blocks rather than reinventing them — Digg and AI Sweden exist precisely so you don't have to start alone.

The strategy's real contribution is permission. It tells public sector leaders that starting small, sharing infrastructure, and being honest about limits is not timid — it is the strategy. After a decade of watching ambitious AI plans stall on the unglamorous details, I find that quietly encouraging.

*Reza Bazargan is a Senior Data Strategist at Governo Insikt AB, where he helps public sector organisations turn data and AI ambitions into projects that actually ship.*

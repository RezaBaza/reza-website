A few years ago I sat in a meeting where someone proudly presented a slide with twenty-three KPIs on it. Twenty-three. Revenue, churn, page views, customer satisfaction, server uptime — all crammed onto one chart in different colours. When I asked which one mattered most that quarter, nobody could answer. They had metrics. What they didn't have was a framework.

That distinction sounds like consultant-speak, but it's actually simple, and getting it right changes how a whole organisation works. So let me try to explain what a KPI framework really means, without the buzzwords.

## A KPI is just a number that tells you something

Let's start at the bottom. KPI stands for Key Performance Indicator. Strip away the importance everyone attaches to those three words, and a KPI is just a number you've decided to watch because it tells you whether things are going well or badly.

If you run a café, the number of coffees sold per day is a KPI. So is the average wait time at the counter. Neither is complicated. The "key" part simply means you've chosen it on purpose, because it reflects something you care about.

The problem starts when people collect numbers without deciding why. That's how you end up with twenty-three of them and no idea what to do.

## A framework is how the numbers connect

A framework is the structure that links your metrics to each other and, more importantly, to what you're actually trying to achieve.

Think of it as three layers.

At the top is your goal — the thing you genuinely want. For a Swedish municipality, that might be "shorter waiting times for elderly home care". For a SaaS company like the one I used to work at, it might be "more teams using the product every week".

In the middle are the outcomes that drive that goal. Shorter waiting times depend on things like how quickly applications are processed and how many staff are available. More weekly usage depends on whether new users find value in their first session.

At the bottom are the day-to-day numbers people can actually influence — applications processed per week, time to first meaningful action in the product, number of staff sick days.

A framework is what holds these layers together so that when someone improves a small number at the bottom, you can see how it moves the big goal at the top. Without that connection, you're just measuring activity and hoping it adds up to something.

## A concrete example

Imagine a region — say Region Skåne — that wants to reduce missed appointments in primary care. The goal is clear: fewer no-shows, because each empty slot is wasted capacity.

The outcome that drives this might be "patients receive a reminder they actually act on". And the day-to-day KPI underneath could be "percentage of patients who confirm their appointment after an SMS reminder".

Now the framework does its job. If the confirmation rate goes up but no-shows don't fall, you've learned something real: maybe people confirm and then forget anyway, so the timing of the reminder is wrong. The numbers are talking to each other. You're not just watching a dashboard tick over; you're testing whether your assumptions about the world are correct.

That's the part most people miss. A good framework isn't a reporting tool. It's a thinking tool.

## Fewer numbers, chosen deliberately

In my experience, the strongest frameworks are also the smallest. When I worked with product teams, the ones that moved fastest usually tracked one main number — sometimes called a "North Star" — and a handful of supporting ones beneath it. Everything else was available if you went looking, but it wasn't on the wall demanding attention.

This matters because attention is the scarcest resource in any organisation. Every metric you put in front of people is a small instruction: *this is what we care about*. Put twenty-three instructions up, and you've effectively given none.

There's a quieter benefit too. When a framework is small and well-connected, people across departments can actually talk to each other. The caseworker, the analyst, and the department head can all point at the same number and agree on what it means. I've seen that shared language do more for an organisation than any new piece of software.

## A simple test

If you want to know whether you have a real framework or just a pile of metrics, try this. Pick any number you report regularly and ask: *if this moves, what bigger thing does it move, and what would I do about it?*

If you can answer cleanly, you have a framework. If you find yourself saying "well, it's good to keep an eye on," that number is probably decoration.

None of this requires advanced analytics. It requires deciding what you care about, and being honest about whether your numbers actually reflect it. The maths is the easy part. The clarity is the work.
